A group chat is not a conversation. It is, structurally, a *congregation*. There's a liturgy (inside jokes), a priesthood (who sets the tone), a heretical fringe (the lurkers who never post), and an Inferno for the apostate (whoever left on read or, God forbid, "took a break").

I started noticing this when i tried to leave a group chat last spring. I said, kindly, "i'm stepping back, love you all." The reaction was disproportionately grief-stricken. Three DMs asked if i was okay. One person sent a long email. Nobody asks that when you stop going to a cafe.

## The structural parallels

- **Liturgy**: the in-jokes. Memes matter not because they're funny but because they're *ours*. Refusing to laugh is heresy.
- **Sacred time**: someone always posts first in the morning. Someone else is "always up at 2am." Roles crystalize.
- **Conversion**: introducing a new member feels like baptizing them. The chat's collective taste has to bend around the new arrival.
- **Excommunication**: the muted chat. The "we should start a new one without paul."
- **Tithes**: your attention. Daily. Fragile. The minute you stop responding, someone says "yo haven't heard from you."

## Why this matters

The chat isn't bad. Small-scale consistent contact is one of the few things that protects mental health in an atomized world. Religions, when small and chosen, do the same.

But *institutionalized* chats — the 40-person high-school hangovers, the 20-message-a-day rotation — those cost something. They cost the same thing institutional religion costs the occasional heretic: **your authentic interior life**.

You start filtering your real opinions through what the chat will tolerate. You post what will get a "lmaooo," not what you actually think. Over the course of a year, that's a slow amputation of anything weird.

## The uncool suggestion

Try muting your biggest chat for a week without announcing it. Watch what your brain does with the reclaimed horizontal space. Some of you will discover you've been writing a novel in the back of your head for six months and never noticed because someone was always typing.

If they ask where you went — and the good ones will — tell them the truth: "i had to put the phone down long enough to miss it for the right reasons."

That's not apostasy. That's just becoming a person again.
